Course D — Robot Town: Amending Rules Toward a KPI

⬅ Back to Course A → Course A — Robot Delivery

Audience: Students who have completed Course A (CADL/SoS-DSL basics). Course B is not required.

Time: 120–150 minutes, plus optional stretch goals.

You will leave with: KPIs written by a human in natural language, the LLM's amendment proposals and decision requests, the human's decisions with their reasons (in prose and in GSN), and the versions of the CADL rules and of the constitution that resulted, period by period.

0. What this course demonstrates​

Courses A and B write a contract once and check a simulation against it. In Course D the contract itself changes while the system runs. Three delivery companies (A, B, C; two robots each) share the 11-node road network of the Raspberry Pi Mouse simulator. After each simulated hour (one period), amendments to the rules are considered.

A human, an LLM and a set of mechanical gates share the work:

WhoDoes what
Human (the service operator)Writes the service KPIs in natural language; the KPIs may change over time. Answers the LLM's decision requests and records the reason and the outcome, in prose or in GSN
LLMTurns the KPI into measurable metrics and proposes amendments to rules, regulation and contracts toward it. When a question cannot clearly be settled, or when human stakeholders must agree, it does not decide: it asks the human and supplies what the decision needs
GatesCheck, constitution, improvement measured on the same random seeds, and the companies' vote. The LLM's own account is never trusted; only measured values count

The exercise lives in the public repository cadl-robot-town. It needs neither Unity nor SUMO: the town is a small Python simulation, and the norms are judged by the same reference runtime as Course A (contract_engine.py, copied from cadl-raspimouse-simulator).

About the recorded LLM output

§4–§8 can be followed without an API key by replaying the records in examples/steward-d-en/ (English) and examples/steward-goodhart/. They are records of real runs with Claude Opus 5.5 on 2026-11-18 (cadl 0.3.36); examples/steward-d/ holds a run in Japanese with the same KPI, which parts from the English one in period 5 (§10). The answers to the decision requests (answers.yaml) were written by Claude playing the human operator when the records were made; from §4 on, answer them yourself. Every number shown is measured by the gates and the simulation on replay. §10 shows how to run a real LLM with your own key.

2. Read the rules, the contract and the regulation (15 min)​

Open cadl/robot_town.cadl. The town has two contracts.

  • DELIVERY_SLA: one instance per order. Pick up within 120 s and deliver within 120 s of pickup, or the order becomes Late. This is the promise to customers.
  • ROAD_USE: one instance each time a robot stops on the road to wait for its next order.
    - id: DELIVERY_SLA
      parties: [TOWN_STEWARD, "ROBOT[*]"]
      guarantee:
        - "pickup_time <= 120s"         # @amend:pickup_deadline
        - "dropoff_time <= 120s"        # @amend:dropoff_deadline
      lifecycle:
        ...
          - id: pickup
            from: Assigned
            to:   PickedUp
            on:   "ROBOT[i].status == PickedUp"
            deadline: 120s            # @amend:pickup_deadline
            on_violation: { transition: Late, severity: Major }
        ...
    - id: ROAD_USE
      parties: [TOWN_STEWARD, "ROBOT[*]", COUNCIL]
      authority:
        decision_holder: COUNCIL
      incentives:
        type: sanction
        lambda: 0.5                   # @amend:sanction_lambda
      lifecycle:
        states: [Holding, Overstay, Released, Evicted]
        initial: Holding
        terminal: [Released, Evicted]
        transitions:
          - id: release
            from: [Holding, Overstay]
            to:   Released
            on:   "ROBOT[i] -> TOWN_STEWARD : node_released"
            deadline: 60s             # @amend:hold_limit
            on_violation: { transition: Overstay, severity: Minor }
      monitors:
        - id: blocking_guard
          observe: "ROBOT[i].blocking"
          sampling: periodic(1000ms)
          rule: "ROBOT.blocking >= 3 AND state IN [Holding, Overstay]"  # @amend:block_k
          on_match: { transition: Evicted, severity: Major }

(Excerpt, simplified.)

The two lifecycles, as state machines:

A line tagged # @amend:<name> holds a parameter that may be amended. When several lines carry the same name (the pickup guarantee and the pickup deadline), they must all hold the same value. Which layer a parameter belongs to and how far it may move is set by constitution.yaml:

amendable:
  hold_limit:       {layer: rule,     min: 10, max: 120, step: 10}   # seconds a robot may wait on the road
  block_k:          {layer: rule,     min: 1,  max: 4,   step: 1}    # robots waiting behind it before eviction
  sanction_lambda:  {layer: rule,     min: 0.0, max: 1.0, step: 0.5} # sanction points per violation (Minor 1, Major 3)
  to_central:       {layer: rule, safety: true, ...}                  # operating-mode switch (§9)
  pickup_deadline:  {layer: contract, min: 60, max: 180, step: 30}   # promised seconds to pickup
  dropoff_deadline: {layer: contract, min: 60, max: 180, step: 30}   # promised seconds to delivery
invariants:
  min_company_on_time_rate: 0.6
vote:
  quorum: 2
regulation:
  invariants.min_company_on_time_rate: {min: 0.5, max: 0.95, step: 0.05}
  vote.quorum:                         {min: 1,   max: 3,    step: 1}

(Excerpt, simplified.)

There are three layers of amendment:

LayerCoversWho decides
RuleROAD_USE and the operating-mode switching conditionsadopted automatically if it passes every gate; the switching conditions (safety: true) are decided by the human
ContractDELIVERY_SLA (the promise to customers)customers have no vote, so the human decides
Regulationvalues in the constitution (invariants, quorum)the human decides

The three companies behave differently (town/policies.py): A always returns to its garage; B waits on the road for its next order but leaves 5 s before hold_limit, and does not wait on the road at all if a single waiting robot triggers eviction (block_k of 1); with no sanctions (sanction_lambda of 0) a violation costs it nothing, so it waits as long as C does. C waits until an order comes or it is evicted; at the highest sanction weight (sanction_lambda of 1.0) it leaves 15 s after the time limit. CADL's check keeps the sanction weight between 0 and 1, and an amendment moves it in steps of 0.5; §3 shows the companies at 0.5. A robot stopped at an intersection blocks everyone who wants to pass through it.

The road network has 11 nodes and 17 roads (town/world.py). The garages of A, B and C are nodes 10, 9 and 4 (yellow). A robot that waits on the road waits at the central intersection 8 (purple), the most convenient place to wait.

4. Write the KPI (15 min)​

The human's first job is to write the KPI. In English it goes in kpi.en.md:

## From period 1

Deliver as many orders as possible across the town within the times we promised customers
(120 seconds from order to pickup, 120 seconds from pickup to delivery). No company's customers
should be kept waiting badly.

## From period 5

The quiet season begins and orders fall. On-time delivery still matters, but to keep the
companies cooperating, no single company should gain or lose because of how the road is used.

A ## From period k heading says when a KPI takes effect (the Japanese file, kpi.md, uses ## 第 k 期から). The loop re-reads the file at the start of every period, so you may edit it between periods. --lang en makes the loop's own text and the LLM's writing English.

A natural-language KPI cannot rank proposals by itself. The LLM rewrites it as a combination of the metrics in town/kpi.py (on-time deliveries, the on-time rate, late deliveries, unfinished orders, waiting times, blocked time, the lowest company's on-time rate, the spread of the companies' utilities, total sanctions, and so on). This is the measure: a main goal (improvement is measured by it), watched goals (the human is told if they get worse by more than the noise) and conditions (which every amendment must keep).

This replays what Claude Opus 5.5 actually wrote on 2026-11-18. When a KPI has a single reading and no concern, the LLM uses its measure without asking. Here the KPI gives no level for "kept waiting badly", and the LLM returned three measures with four concerns. Only measure 2 has a target (a level such as "at most 240 s"), and the LLM marked it as a candidate for the human to confirm: the request asks the LLM not to invent levels the KPI does not state. The machine checks the metric names and the shape of every measure the LLM writes; one that uses an unknown metric is dropped from the choices.

worst_company_p95_wait_s is the longest of the companies' 95th-percentile waits. A wait is the time from order to delivery; an order still undelivered at the end of the period counts up to the end of the period. python -m town.kpi lists every metric.

After you pick a number you are asked for a reason and for assumptions (GSN Assumptions; any number of lines, an empty line ends). The recorded answers (examples/steward-d-en/answers.yaml) were written by Claude playing the operator when the record was made:

  assumptions:

Writing the measure and its targets yourself​

The KPI's sentences alone do not say what to measure or how far is far enough. If a section of the KPI file has an Evaluation: list, town.steward uses it as the measure as written and does not ask the LLM to read the section (a Japanese example is examples/kpi-written/kpi.md).

## From period 1

At least 95% of deliveries across the town should arrive within the promised times. In every
company, at most 5% of customers should wait longer than the promised total of 240 seconds.

Evaluation:
- goal: on_time_rate >= 0.95
- watch: worst_company_p95_wait_s <= 240
- guard: min_company_on_time_rate >= 0.8
LineMeaning
goalexactly one; improvement is measured by it. A value makes it a target
watchif it gets worse by more than the noise, the human is asked (KPI conflict). A value makes it a target
guardneeds a value; every amendment must keep it (a proposal that breaks it raises a KPI conflict)

In every period the loop shows whether the current rules meet each target and guard ("KPI status"), and the proposal table shows whether each proposal would. Targets are for display; they do not decide whether a proposal is adopted.

python -m town.kpi my-kpi.en.md     # check how each section is read

A request contains the question; why the LLM is not deciding; for each option what happens and who gains or loses; the LLM's recommendation; and what the LLM does not know. The options are the proposals that raised a reason, the proposal that could be adopted automatically (if any) and "keep"; the LLM cannot add options. The table of numbers is the gates' measurement, not the LLM's prose.

Customers do not vote. Each company votes by its own utility, so the customers who gain from fewer late deliveries have no voice in the vote. The LLM weighed that against overriding two companies and recommended keeping the rules. The recorded answer adopts proposal 2, overturning the vote:

  choice: "2"
  reason: The intersections are a road shared by the three companies; waiting there is no company's right. On-time deliveries rise on all 5 seeds, late deliveries fall from 10.4 to 1.2 and the worst company's p95 wait from 273 s to 95.6 s, so adopt it against B's and C's votes. A recommendation is the output of one request. The LLM laid out the material and showed that each choice had reasons; the human decided.

The record of decisions​

Each decision goes into two files in runs/<name>/:

  • decisions.md: the prose record — for each decision, the measure, the LLM's assessment, why the human was asked, the LLM's recommendation and unknowns, and the human's choice and reason.
  • gsn.yaml: a GSN (Goal Structuring Notation) tree, with a Mermaid diagram in gsn.md.
GSN elementWhat goes in it
Goalthe KPI written by the human (one per version)
Strategythe decision of a period (the amendment adopted, or no change)
Solutionthe measurement on the same seeds (p<k>-evidence.md)
Contextthe measure of the KPI; the period's environment
Assumptionassumptions written by the human
Justificationthe human's reason (or, for an automatic decision, its condition)

The tree in gsn.md has this shape. Solid arrows are supported by; dotted arrows are in context of.

Reasons may be written in prose or as GSN elements; in the --answers YAML, list elements (each with type: and text:) under gsn:. For example (the recorded answer to D2 gives its reason in prose):

D2:
  choice: keep
  gsn:
    - type: justification
      text: Only 2 of 5 seeds improve; do not cut C's utility from 56.3 to 40.1 for an uncertain gain
    - type: context
      text: In D1 we decided to re-measure C's utility and the road violations

From the next period on, earlier decisions and the human's reasons are passed to the LLM. §6 shows how that changes its proposals and recommendations.

12. Research exercise: speed of amendment and transition disruption (optional, several hours)​

To reach the KPI sooner, it is tempting to change the rules a lot at once. If the people who follow the rules are humans, large sudden changes confuse them, and until they adjust they keep acting on the old rules. Robots read the new rules at once and are not confused — but that does not mean the transition causes no problems. This exercise asks how the trade-off between reaching the KPI quickly and the size and frequency of rule changes depends on whether humans or robots follow the rules.

The hands-on already gives some clues:

  • The constitution's "one parameter by one step per amendment" (step in constitution.yaml) is an upper limit on the speed of change.
  • In the six periods of the rule-based council used in §8 (seasons-d), the sanction weight went from 0.5 to 1 in period 1, then hold_limit went 60 → 70 → 80 s in the busy season, with the default step of 10 s, and nothing changed in the quiet season. This is not overshoot: it follows the demand that scenario.yaml sets for each period. A rule that tracks a changing environment moves even when every step is right, so separating tracking from overshoot is part of the exercise.
  • In §9, in a period with a rush, switching the operating mode without waiting for the road to clear broke the safety invariant about 1.4 times per period, with robots only. When the rules change, orders and waits begun under the old version are still running.

13. Record the decisions (90–120 min, plus an optional exercise)​

The records of §4–§10 (decisions.md, gsn.yaml) are written for people. To show afterwards who agreed to each change and who decided it, on what basis and why, the record has to be checked by a program as well. From cadl 0.3.37, town.steward and town.council also write runs/<name>/decisions.jsonl, a decision record of Appendix L (decisions 0.2 from cadl 0.3.38 and cadl-robot-town v0.4.0; the CADL files of the versions from cadl-robot-town v0.5.0; the delegations, decisions 0.3, from cadl 0.3.39 and cadl-robot-town v0.6.0):

  • one line per entry: objectives, proposals, measurements, checks, votes, decision requests, recommendations, decisions, versions and follow-ups, each naming the earlier entries it rests on (basis);
  • the parties, their roles and the layers on which each decides: the operator (human, the rules and the contract), the regulator (human, the regulation, that is, the values of the constitution), the LLM (llm), the steward or the council (tool, which adopts rule changes by itself when they pass every check, on behalf of the operator), the companies A, B, C (agent, voters) and the customers (organisation, affected, without a vote);
  • who let whom decide in their place: a delegation from the operator to the steward or the council (the rule layer, by its own rule) and, when the LLM decides for a person, to the LLM (the layers given with --delegate);
  • each version of the rules, the first included, with its CADL file v<n>.cadl attached: the rules with the amendment extension of Appendix K written in (the amendable parameters, their places, ranges and steps, and who decides each layer);
  • the same evidence from every party, whether a person, the LLM or a tool: a reason, assumptions, what it does not know, and predictions;
  • for each follow-up, what fulfils it: for a promise to measure again (remeasure), a measurement under the same conditions as the one it re-checks, which the steward makes in the next period; for a promise to propose an amendment (propose), a later proposal; for a question (ask), a later decision, which the steward asks for when the question falls due, and for every open question in the last period;
  • on every line the hash chain of the audit log of Appendix G.
cadl decisions-verify runs/<name>/decisions.jsonl

With the version files next to the record, the verifier also checks the versions and their order, that every proposal moves a parameter of the version in force within its range and step, and who decided each change and whether the votes reached the quorum (L-9 to L-11). The verifier reports errors (L-1 to L-11: the record cannot be relied on as it stands) and findings for review (an override of the votes, a decision against the recommendation, a decision made on behalf of another party outside every delegation, a prediction that a later vote or measurement contradicted, a claim to fulfil a follow-up that does not meet it, a follow-up not fulfilled, …). A finding is not an error: the record should show who took the decision and why. The page Decision Evidence of CADL Explorer shows the same record as a timeline, the trail of each decision, the evidence of each party, the follow-ups, and a GSN derived from the record.

Exercises 13-1 to 13-6 cover amendments of the rules (ROAD_USE) and of the contract (DELIVERY_SLA, the times promised to customers); exercise 13-7 adds the regulation; exercises 13-8 to 13-13, optional, take the record further.

Several of them edit a copy of a record. The chain must then be recomputed from the edited entries:

# rechain.py — rewrite a decision record after editing its entries
import json
from cadl.decisions import DecisionChain

def load(path):
    rows = [json.loads(l) for l in open(path, encoding="utf-8")]
    strip = lambda d: {k: v for k, v in d.items() if k not in ("seq", "prev", "digest")}
    return strip(rows[0]), [strip(r) for r in rows[1:] if not r.get("decisions_end")]

def save(path, header, entries):
    c = DecisionChain(run_id=header["run_id"], format=header["decisions_format"])
    lines = [c.header(header["model"], header["parties"], header["started"],
                      header.get("time_base"), header.get("previous"))]
    lines += [c.entry(e) for e in entries] + [c.end()]
    open(path, "w", encoding="utf-8").write("\n".join(lines) + "\n")

Copy the version files (v<n>.cadl) next to each edited copy, so that the verifier also checks the versions.

Exercise 13-12: a decision record of another SoS (optional)​

A decision record does not depend on the robot town. Take the SoS you chose in Course C, or the one below, and write by hand the record of one amendment of one of its rules: an objective, a measurement of the rules in force, a proposal, its measurement, the votes, a decision with a reason, assumptions and unknowns, and a follow-up that says what fulfils it.

An example: shared chargers in an apartment building

Four households share two chargers. The management company decides the booking rule (the rule layer) and also measures (it reads the booking log); an LLM proposes; the households vote; visitors are affected and do not vote.

14. What the specification cannot express yet​

Building this exercise exposed five gaps in CADL 0.3. They were all there up to cadl 0.3.31; cadl 0.3.32 adds syntax or IR output for each of them, as the items below say, while the exercise itself still keeps its own files and lets the simulator apply the rules:

  • Up to cadl 0.3.31, cadl sim-ir did not emit sos.transitions[*].safety_invariant into the IR (cadl check did check it as an expression), so the exercise reads it from the checked source file. From cadl 0.3.32 the IR carries it as written in transitions[*].safety_invariant (Appendix A, A.7.1).
  • Up to cadl 0.3.31 an operating mode (regime) was only a name, with no syntax to bind parameter values or contracts to it. From cadl 0.3.32 the block regimes: binds, per regime, values of alpha, beta and lambda of a contract and of entries of context.environment, and the contracts that are active or suspended (Appendix A, A.7.1). cadl sim-ir writes the bindings of every regime, cadl verify reports a conflict between two contracts that no regime has in force together as a warning instead of a failure, the generated Python code monitors a contract only in the regimes in which it is in force, and the stochastic model extension takes the bound values as the parameter values of its designs (Appendix H, H.6). CADL can now say that centralized mode has bays (for example environment.bays: 3) and which contracts hold in it; how robots use the bays and the exclusive edges is still decided by the simulator.
  • Up to cadl 0.3.31, which layer (rule or contract) a parameter belongs to, and who decides its amendment, could be written only in constitution.yaml and in # @amend: comments. From cadl 0.3.32 the amendment extension (amendment: 0.1, Appendix K, K.3) declares each amendable parameter in the model: its layer, the actor or role that decides (for example COUNCIL by vote with a quorum of 2, or a human OPERATOR), min, max, step, how many steps one amendment may move it, and at:, the places of the model that hold the value. cadl check rejects an unknown actor, a value outside the range or off the steps, and a place that does not hold the value. The exercise still reads constitution.yaml and the comments; from cadl-robot-town v0.5.0 it writes the declaration into the file of each version that the decision record attaches (§13), and the verifier checks the proposals and the decisions against it.
  • Up to cadl 0.3.31 there was no syntax for when a version of the rules takes effect, or for which version applies to contract instances already running (transitional provisions; §12, Exercise 12-3). From cadl 0.3.32 a version gives sos.version, the version it replaces, the period or time from which it applies, and per contract whether running instances keep the old version until they end or switch to the new one, at once or when they are in given states (Appendix K, K.4). Switching is the case to which on_amendment: of the norms extension applies (Appendix F, F.6); an instance that keeps the old version is not amended. cadl amend-diff OLD NEW lists the parameters and contracts that changed between two versions and reports every change that the declared amendability does not allow. No tool applies versions while the town runs: the simulator still decides that.
  • Up to cadl 0.3.31 a metrics target was a single expression, with no syntax for a KPI that changes over time, for how a KPI is measured, or to link the record of human decisions (GSN) to a version of the rules. From cadl 0.3.32 a metric may give schedule: (a target from a period, a time or a version on), measure: (for example one period, five seeds, the mean), and a version lists the decision records it rests on (decisions.md#d1, gsn.yaml#S2), which parameters, targets and measurements name (Appendix K, K.5). These are declarations: the KPI itself, in natural language, and the records stay in kpi.md, decisions.md and gsn.yaml. From cadl 0.3.37 the records themselves have a format, the decision records of Appendix L, which the exercise writes as decisions.jsonl and cadl decisions-verify checks (§13); from 0.3.38 (decisions 0.2) a record says on which layers each party decides and what fulfils each follow-up, and from 0.3.39 (decisions 0.3) who let whom decide in their place.

Up to cadl 0.3.11, an unreadable value such as deadline: soon passed cadl check and became null in the IR; from 0.3.12 it is an error. The exercise also rejects rules whose IR lacks a value the town needs, so the check gate works even with an older compiler.

Summary​

  • The KPI is written by a human in natural language and may change over time. The LLM turns it into a measure, but when it can be read several ways or relies on a proxy, the human chooses.
  • The LLM proposes amendments to rules, regulation and contracts toward the KPI. Matters needing stakeholders' agreement, the promise to customers, regulation, safety, conflicts within the KPI and uncertain improvements it does not decide: it hands them to the human with the measurements, who gains and who loses, and what it does not know.
  • The human's decisions and reasons are kept, in prose and in GSN, alongside the versions, and change the LLM's next proposals and requests.
  • What finally backs an adoption is the gates — check, constitution, measured improvement and the parties' agreement — together with the reasons the human left.
